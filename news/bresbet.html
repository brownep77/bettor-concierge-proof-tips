BresBet shut after a licence freeze. Some punters are still waiting.

BresBet is not a bank crash. The Gambling Commission stopped its licence, the firm shut down, and some customers are still waiting for money they were told would arrive in days. That is the story. The bigger lesson is older. When a UK bookie fails, you only get paid if the firm still has cash, or if a bigger firm buys the customer list. The official protection labels help less than most people think. And money already stuck on a bet is not protected at all. Football Index showed that, at a cost of more than £100 million.

What happened at BresBet

On 28 August 2026 the UK Gambling Commission froze the licences of BresBet and its sister firm, Bet St George. It said it suspected failures on safer gambling and anti money laundering rules. It opened a formal review. It has not published proof that those failures happened.

Nic Brereton set up BresBet in 2021 and also ran Bet St George, which launched in 2026. BresBet got its own UK licence in 2025 after years of running as a white label brand. A week after the freeze, on 4 September, both firms handed back their licences and told customers they were stopping.

This is not, as of late September 2026, a formal bankruptcy. There is no administrator and no liquidation. The Commission pushed them out, and they left. That matters. The official labels (not protected, medium, high) are built for a firm that cannot pay its bills. They have not been tested here in that way. What still applies is a basic rule: if a licensed firm stops trading, it must not leave customers worse off than they need to be.

Customers were told to claim balances over £1 by 30 September. Ante post bets would be cancelled and the stake sent back. BresBet had first said already placed bets would stand and be paid as normal. That did not happen. Open bets were cancelled. In theory you get your stake back, not a win at the price you took.

That theory is already slipping. On 25 September the Racing Post said customers had been waiting almost a month, even after emails promising five to seven working days.

Shaun Perry had accounts with both brands. Bet St George paid him. BresBet still owed him £300.12. The firm emailed to say it would pay within five days. Three or four weeks later the money had not arrived. Follow up emails got an automatic reply. A Facebook message to Brereton went unanswered. His account looked unused.

Jamie Howlett asked on 5 September for a refund of two £100 ante post bets on Arsenal to win the Premier League. On 11 September BresBet said a rush of withdrawals might take up to seven working days. When nothing came, a later reply said it could not give a time. After that, automatic messages. Howlett contacted the Commission twice. Staff said it was hard because the firm no longer had a licence, though they said they were watching. "It's not about the money. It's not a huge amount, but it's owed to me," he told the Racing Post.

The Commission still says it expects the business to have a plan and to make sure customers are not left in the lurch. Once a licence is handed back, that is harder to enforce. Several customers have already talked about small claims court.

Some refunds have gone out. Bet St George paid Perry. Others are still waiting. Whether this ends as a slow payout or as money that never arrives will be clear after the 30 September deadline, not before.

What funds protection really means

Money in a UK betting account is not like money in a bank. There is no government safety net. The Commission will not repay you if the bookie goes under. The firm must tell you what, if anything, would happen if it went bust. From 31 October 2025, any firm whose funds are "not protected" must remind you every six months how much it holds and that the money is not protected. You have to confirm you have seen that message before you can use the money to bet.

There are four official levels.

Not protected, no split accounts. Only allowed for some shop based and small remote set ups. No protection if the firm goes bust.

Not protected, split accounts. This is the legal minimum for every online firm that holds customer money. Your cash sits in a bank account separate from the firm's own account. That sounds safer than it is. If the firm goes bust, that money is still treated as the company's. You join the queue of people the company owes, behind banks and other secured lenders. A split account is bookkeeping. It is not a lock.

Medium protection. The firm has set up extra steps, such as a special type of trust or insurance. The idea is that customer money should go back to customers if the firm fails. The Commission, and the firms themselves, still say there is no hard guarantee.

High protection. The money sits in a formal trust, legally separate from the company. An independent trustee controls it. An outside auditor checks it. This is the strongest label the Commission allows, and the best chance of getting a cash balance back.

Two limits sit under every label.

First, "customer funds" means cash sitting in your account, plus some bonuses already earned but not yet paid. It does not mean money already placed on a bet that has not been settled. Open bets are outside the scheme. Ante post bets are outside the scheme. That is how Football Index customers lost a huge sum while a much smaller cash pot was paid back. Howlett's two £100 Arsenal bets sit in that same gap. Even a High rating would not automatically cover them.

Second, the Commission does not force anyone to offer Medium or High protection. Its stance is: tell people the truth, and let them choose. The Horseracing Bettors Forum has said it is still "stunned" that "no protection" is allowed. The October 2025 reminder rule made things clearer. It did not make the safety net stronger.

UK firms that failed, and who got paid

There is no single official list of every UK licensed brand that ever shut. Many just left the market. They were not broke. The useful split is three groups: firms that went under and customers lost money; firms that went under but a buyer stepped in; and firms that left while still able to pay.

Customers lost most or all

Sporting Options (2004). An early betting exchange went under owing about £3.6 million. Client accounts held less than £100,000. The firm itself did not repay people. Betfair stepped in. Anyone owed up to £1,000 got a full credit. Anyone owed more got £1,000 or 20 per cent, whichever was larger. That was a rescue, not the protection system working.

Canbet (2014). An Australian bookie with a UK licence delayed payouts for months, blamed "technical upgrades", and kept taking deposits. Player money was used to run the business. Balances were not paid. Millions went with the firm.

BetButler (2014). The Commission took away the licence. Money was not locked away from the firm's other bills. The customer list was sold to Momaco for £1. There is no solid public record that players were repaid. Bodugi had been bought by BetButler shortly before. Those balances were moved across, then a play through rule was added, then BetButler itself collapsed.

Win Cash Live (2016). Shut. No good reports that customers were repaid.

666Bet (2015). Mixed. Some players were paid. Some were not. After pressure from the Commission the firm later started sending money back through Skrill, with a cut off date. After that date unpaid customers would have to go to court.

Football Index / BetIndex (2021). The biggest customer loss in modern UK gambling. The site sold itself as a football stock market. The "shares" were legally open bets. When it collapsed, that book was worth about £90 to £124 million. Cash sitting in a protected pot was only about £3.2 to £4.5 million. Administrators paid those cash balances to people who claimed in time. The share book was not "customer funds". Liquidators later spoke of a return of about 1 to 5 per cent on the open bets. The government refused to compensate people from public money. An independent review criticised both the Gambling Commission and the Financial Conduct Authority. Campaigners were still fighting years later. Football Index had a Medium rating. That rating covered the cash pot. It did not cover the product most customers thought they owned.

Older poker rooms (Purple Lounge, Full Tilt's UK facing books, Everleaf, 5050 Poker, and PKR before it was rescued). Split accounts did not save deposits. Where money came back, it came from a buyer or a rival, not from the label on the licence.

Customers were paid, usually because someone bought the book

Sun Bets (2018). A planned shutdown by News UK and Tabcorp, not a cash crash. Balances were paid. Some ante post bets were paid. Stakes were sent back on the rest.

BetBright (2019). 888 bought the business. Balances were paid. Cheltenham Festival ante post was paid. Other open bets were cancelled and stakes sent back.

MoPlay / Addison Global (2020). Regulators froze the licences after the firm could not pay its bills. The terms said customer money would not be treated as separate from company money. Betfred bought about 45,000 UK and Ireland accounts and paid the balances. Open bets were cancelled and stakes sent back. That was a purchase, not a payout from a protection scheme.

PKR (mid 2010s). Funds were not protected. PokerStars bought the customer list and paid back around $2 million. Again, a rescue.

Firms that left while still able to pay

A long list of brands left the UK without going bust: Royal Panda, MansionBet, STS, Sport Nation, Red Zone, EnergyBet and LVBET, BetBull, Genting Bet, The Pools, parts of Vbet's UK business, and NRG Bet in May 2026. The usual pattern is a published deadline, support left open for a few weeks, and open bets either paid up to a cut off or cancelled with the stake returned.

NRG Bet is a clear example. It stopped new business on 21 April 2026, gave customers until 20 May to withdraw, and said it would honour open and ante post bets, sending later wins to the original payment method. That is what a clean exit looks like. BresBet is being judged against that standard. So far it is falling short.

TGP Europe's 2025 licence handover after a large fine, and InTouch Games' 2023 handover after a freeze, sit in the same group: forced out, but still expected to pay. The Commission's own closing down guidance is simple. Settle bets. Tell customers. Keep a way open for unclaimed money. Treat about four weeks after closure as a fair window.

How safe is the current UK market?

Better than 2004. Uneven. Weaker than the words "UKGC licensed" sound.

High protection sits where you would hope. Flutter's Paddy Power, Betfair Exchange and Betfair Sportsbook are High, as is PokerStars. Entain's Ladbrokes, Coral, bwin and Sportingbet are High. So are Betway, BetVictor, Betano, Smarkets, Matchbook, Hollywoodbets, SBK and a few others. Those firms use independent trusts with a trustee and an auditor. If one of those groups failed tomorrow, cash balances would have the strongest claim the Commission's system allows.

Medium is where a lot of everyday UK betting actually sits. bet365, Sky Bet, William Hill, Betfred, BoyleSports, Unibet, 888sport, Virgin Bet, QuinnBet, Tote, BetMGM, LeoVegas, Livescore Bet, Grosvenor, 32Red, 10Bet and Spreadex all say Medium. Betfred's own page is clear: a separate bank account, a written note from the bank that the money will not be used to clear the firm's other debts, inside and outside audits, and the usual line that there is no hard promise every penny will be repaid. Sky Bet is part of Flutter, and it is still only Medium. Britain's biggest online sportsbook is Medium. Medium means extra steps exist. It also means those steps can fail.

Not protected (split accounts only) is still legal. The Horseracing Bettors Forum's June 2025 list includes Betgoodwin, Geoff Banks, Karamba, McBookie, Star Sports, Planet Sport Bet, Rhino Bet and PricedUp. PricedUp's own terms say it in plain words: funds are held separately in bank accounts, investment accounts and payment firm reserves, and "these funds are not protected in the event of insolvency." A longer list of smaller and white label brands sit in the same place. Many run on the same kind of platform BresBet used. Around the 2023 to 2024 rule review the Commission's own figures put only about 3 per cent of customer funds in unprotected set ups. That was still more than £21 million, held by the kind of smaller firm most likely to fail.

Four holes are still there.

Open bets are left out at every level. That is not a small print point. It is how Football Index went from a Medium rated firm to a six year fight. It is also why Howlett's unpaid Arsenal stakes sit outside the scheme.

Firms choose their own label. The Commission makes them publish it. It does not run a live public board of who is High this month.

After a licence is handed back, the Commission has less power. That is the BresBet problem in one line. The regulator can remind a former licensee of its duties. It cannot easily force a bank transfer.

There is still no state payout scheme. History is blunt. Customers get paid when the firm is still able to pay and shuts down cleanly, or when a bigger firm wants the customer list, or when a rival pays to protect the industry's name. When the till is empty, the label on the licence has a poor record. Sporting Options, Canbet, BetButler and the Football Index share book all show that.

What BresBet changes

BresBet is not Football Index. The sums named in public so far are hundreds of pounds, not nine figure losses, and the firms have not gone into administration. It is also not Sun Bets. The refund clock has already run past the firm's own promises. Customer service has collapsed into stock replies. The founder has not answered the racing press.

It is a mid size specialist leaving after a licence freeze, with no buyer, while the regulator that froze it no longer holds a licence it can threaten to take away. The ratings system was not built for that. The closer match is BetButler in 2014: a licence action, then a messy wind down, then customers finding that "email us by this date" is not the same as money in the account.

If you are still owed money, keep every email. Screenshot the balance and the original bets. Send the claim before the 30 September deadline if you have not already. Treat small claims court as a real option, not a threat for show.

If you are choosing where to leave a balance, read the firm's insolvency rating in the terms. Treat "split accounts" as the legal minimum, not a comfort. Prefer High for any sum you would mind losing. Treat an ante post ticket as a promise, not a protected deposit.

The Commission can force a reminder every six months. It cannot make a shut bookmaker pay on time. That gap is what BresBet is now testing.
